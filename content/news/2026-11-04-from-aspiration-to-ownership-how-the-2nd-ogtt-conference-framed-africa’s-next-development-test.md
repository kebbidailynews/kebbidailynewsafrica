  On Tuesday, November 3, 2026, the Congress Hall of the Transcorp Hilton Hotel
  in Abuja filled with former central bankers, sitting ministers, a state
  governor, investors, technologists and young professionals for the second
  Oxford Global Think Tank Leadership Conference. Sessions began early and
  continued through the day, ending with an evening cocktail reception. The
  theme was “Leadership for a Prosperous Future.” Organisers structured the
  programme around three pillars: resilience, innovation and dynamism. The
  practical question that ran through nearly every session was clear. What
  decisions, institutions and investments will it take to turn Africa’s young
  population and abundant resources into prosperity that ordinary people can
  actually feel?


  The gathering took place at a moment when rapid advances in artificial intelligence, shifting global power, demographic change and economic strain are reshaping the options open to African governments. The conference was framed as a space to move from aspiration to concrete action, bringing leaders from government, business, technology, finance and civil society into the same room for sustained discussion.


  The Oxford Global Think Tank is an independent, non-governmental and non-partisan organisation founded by Arunma Oteh. She served as Director-General of the Securities and Exchange Commission from 2010 to 2015 and later as Vice President and Treasurer of the World Bank. OGTT’s mission is to equip public and private sector leaders with research, dialogue and practical ideas for sound policy and stronger institutions. The first conference, held in Abuja in late October 2025 under the theme “Better Leadership for a Better Nigeria,” marked Nigeria’s 65th Independence anniversary and featured OGTT’s maiden report on African minerals. A year later the second edition widened the focus from national leadership questions to Africa’s position in a technology-driven global economy.


  The conference was co-chaired by Muhammadu Sanusi II, the Emir of Kano and former Governor of the Central Bank of Nigeria, and Atedo Peterside, founder of the ANAP Foundation and of Stanbic IBTC. The keynote was delivered by Dr Akinwumi Adesina, former President of the African Development Bank. Abia State Governor Alex Otti gave the public sector keynote. Dr Bosun Tijani, Minister of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy, addressed the AI agenda. Ogechukwu Modie moderated the Public Sector Leaders Session.


  Artificial intelligence formed a central strand of the day. Organisers launched the inaugural OGTT Artificial Intelligence Report, unveiled an AI Virtual Centre and hosted an exhibition of emerging technologies. The report is intended to shape Africa’s AI trajectory, deepen collaboration and help the continent capture value rather than remain a passive market.Adesina’s keynote produced the day’s most widely quoted proposal. He called for a $10 billion Africa AI Frontier Technology Fund to serve as the anchor for mobilising $50 billion over the next decade. Africa, he said, must approach the AI revolution determined “not to follow, but to lead.” Technological ownership, in his view, is now a question of economic sovereignty. With the continent projected to reach 2.5 billion people by 2050, Africa cannot afford to depend indefinitely on innovations built elsewhere. His framework had five clear parts: capture more of the economic value that AI creates; invest heavily in digital and energy infrastructure; move from adopting technology to creating it at home; mobilise large pools of institutional capital; and strengthen political leadership, which he suggested could include dedicated ministers for artificial intelligence.Adesina called for a coalition involving the World Bank, the African Development Bank, African governments and private technology partners. He also argued that Africa’s youthful population must be treated as a genuine demographic asset class capable of inventing, commercialising and exporting advanced technologies. Young Africans, he stressed, need quality education, healthcare, nutrition, technical skills, finance and digital tools so they can start businesses and create jobs rather than wait for formal employment.


  The proposal arrives months after the African Development Bank and the UNDP launched a separate AI 10 Billion Initiative at the Nairobi AI Forum in February 2026, which aims to mobilise up to $10 billion by 2035 across data, computing capacity, skills, trust and capital. Adesina’s fund is a distinct proposal made at the OGTT conference. No financing commitments for it were announced, and the same practical questions that have been asked of the earlier initiative, whether $10 billion can stretch far enough against Africa’s infrastructure needs, will apply here as well.Minister Bosun Tijani grounded the AI conversation in infrastructure and absorptive capacity. Prosperity, he said, depends on a country’s ability to apply knowledge and technology to the resources it already has. He pointed to Nigeria’s telecommunications liberalisation in 2001 as evidence of how policy reform, competition and private investment can transform an economy. AI is the next such opportunity, but countries benefit only if they build connectivity, computing and data infrastructure, skills, research capability and supportive institutions. He cited a 90,000-kilometre open-access fibre network, the 3 Million Technical Talent programme, Africa’s first government-backed large language model and a national AI strategy. These investments, he argued, have positioned Nigeria first in Africa for responsible AI governance. He urged business, academia and policymakers to work together so that emerging technology delivers everyday improvements for citizens.Atedo Peterside supplied the growth arithmetic that underpinned much of the discussion. Nigeria’s growth of about four percent, he said, is inadequate to create the jobs and investment needed to pull people out of poverty. The country should aim for 10 percent annual growth. He traced a steady decline in Nigeria’s investment-to-GDP ratio since 1999 despite the optimism that followed the return to democracy. Investors are discouraged when access to opportunity depends on political connections and when small businesses face harassment from regulators. He was particularly sharp on public spending, warning that inflated contracts and awards to politically connected partners turn procurement into legalised theft. Moving from four percent to ten percent is impossible without tackling corruption and putting public money to productive use.The Emir of Kano acknowledged measurable progress, noting that growth of around 4.5 percent is better than in previous years. He insisted, however, that economic performance must be judged by its effect on people’s lives rather than by headline GDP alone. The depreciation of the naira has dented GDP per capita and household purchasing power, so growth, currency movements and welfare have to be read together. Access to education, healthcare, nutrition and jobs, and the way opportunity is distributed across the country, are the more reliable tests. He cautioned that some gains may be fragile. Foreign portfolio investment attracted by high interest rates can leave when conditions change. Sanusi called for a reform programme that goes beyond fuel subsidy removal and foreign exchange liberalisation to include infrastructure, structural reform, transparency and accountability. He criticised the habit of dismissing public complaints about hardship as opposition, arguing that these concerns deserve serious attention. He also raised the issue of regional imbalance in political and security appointments, stating that inclusion is essential to national cohesion and to perceptions of fairness.


  Governor Alex Otti used the public sector keynote to address Nigeria’s elite directly. Poverty, institutional decay and insecurity, he said, are largely rooted in weak leadership. The elite have a duty to build institutions rather than retreat from them. When influential people put private interest above public service, the result is alienation, hardship and frustration. He pointed to the decline of institutions such as Nigerian Airways and the national shipping line, and the loss of jobs, incomes and national pride that followed. Otti urged the use of AI and other emerging technologies to strengthen government by tracking corruption, monitoring projects, improving civil service performance, raising revenue and encouraging citizen participation. He presented Abia State as a working example: the move to smart schools, the use of renewable energy in health and public institutions, the purchase of electric vehicles for mass transport, computer-based examinations for civil service recruitment and promotion, and the digitisation of land administration. He stated that Abia had issued more Certificates of Occupancy in three years than in the previous decade. These are the governor’s own claims presented at the conference. His closing message was directed at educated and influential Nigerians: abandon political apathy, take part in democratic processes and hold office holders to account.


  Convener Arunma Oteh opened the day by arguing for an ecosystem approach in which government, business, investors and citizens each have defined roles. Her priorities were deeper domestic capital markets with broader public participation, better infrastructure, access to finance, regulatory certainty and skills for young people. African countries, she said, must mobilise their own resources rather than wait for external money. She also made the case for decentralisation, using electricity reforms to illustrate how subnational governments can improve power supply and support economic activity.Taken together, the speeches produced a consistent set of arguments. Leadership and institutions come first. Otti, Peterside and Sanusi all located Nigeria’s difficulties in how decisions are made and enforced, not in any shortage of resources or talent. Growth must be both larger and more inclusive. The gap between four percent and the ten percent Peterside advocates is the difference between managing poverty and beginning to end it. Sanusi’s insistence that growth must register in household welfare gave that argument its practical test. AI is fundamentally an ownership question. Adesina’s fund proposal and Tijani’s infrastructure agenda share the same premise: whoever owns the data, the compute capacity and the applications captures the value. The OGTT AI Report and Virtual Centre are designed to keep that conversation alive after the hall empties. Capital must be local as well as foreign. Oteh’s emphasis on domestic capital markets and Adesina’s call for institutional capital point to the same need for patient money invested at scale. Young people are not a secondary concern; they are the strategy. Every major speaker returned to Africa’s youth as a demographic dividend, a talent pipeline and a political constituency.Conferences are ultimately measured by what follows them. Three developments are worth watching. First, whether Adesina’s $10 billion fund finds institutional sponsors and how it relates to existing initiatives. Second, whether the OGTT AI Report and Virtual Centre produce concrete partnerships, datasets or policy proposals. Third, whether the direct language on procurement, inclusion and the lived experience of hardship translates into any measurable policy response. As Governor Otti observed, the impact of such a gathering is measured by what participants do with the awareness they leave with.


  For readers in Kebbi and across northern Nigeria the debates in Abuja are not abstract. Jobs for young people, the price of food, access to finance for small businesses and the reach of digital skills all sit behind the figures quoted in the Congress Hall. The second OGTT Leadership Conference did not invent new problems. It clarified the quality of decisions required to address the ones already known, and it placed those decisions firmly in the hands of the leaders who were present.
